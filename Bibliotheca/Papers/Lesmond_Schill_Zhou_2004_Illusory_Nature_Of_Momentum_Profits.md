# Lesmond, Schill & Zhou (2004) — The Illusory Nature of Momentum Profits

*The abstract only, with the citation checked against the publisher's record — not the full paper.*

## Why it is here

Claim 1 of [`OBJECTIVE.md`](../../OBJECTIVE.md) — *a positive twelve-month return, skipping the
most recent month, selects stocks whose following month beats the universe average* — where this
paper is what is still open: whether any of it survives inside the liquid quintile. It also bears on
claim 3 — *the top-quintile liquidity screen makes every position exitable in a day at ordinary
volume* — as one of the two sources that word the screen as a cost.

**The paper that argues against this strategy.** It is the most useful note in this folder, and it
is here because of rule 3 of the note convention: a source that says to do the opposite of what we
do stays visible.

## Standard momentum strategies trade disproportionately in high-cost securities

The stocks that generate large relative-strength returns are not a random sample of the market. They
are concentrated among securities with high trading costs — the small, the volatile, the thinly
traded — and the strategy requires frequent trading in exactly those names.

> **For claim 3:** this is a direct attack on our design, and we accept it as one. The strategy
> screens to the **top** liquidity quintile. If momentum profits live where the costs are, the screen
> removes the names that generate the effect, and the liquid book may show little or nothing
> **gross**, before costs are even discussed.
>
> That risk is taken deliberately: **a strategy that only works where it cannot be traded is not a
> strategy.** The screen is not an oversight to be defended, it is the test.

## Trading costs are large enough to prevent profitable execution of the strategy

Once realistic costs are applied, the paper reports that the profits do not survive — the returns are
illusory in the sense that they are not reachable by anyone who has to trade to get them.

> **For claim 1:** this is why the claim has to be evaluated twice, gross and net, and why the bar in
> [`AGENTS.md`](../../AGENTS.md) accepts results net or not at all. It is also why the commission
> model is not a detail to be settled at the end: on this evidence it is the variable that decides
> the answer.

## What it changes

- **Claim 1 is evaluated twice, gross and net**, results are accepted net or not at all, and the
  commission model is settled early rather than at the end — on this evidence it decides the answer.
- **The liquidity screen is the test, not an oversight to defend:** a strategy that only works where
  it cannot be traded is not a strategy.
- **A negative result is specific.** If the liquid-only book has no gross edge, what is falsified is
  *momentum inside the liquid quintile* — not momentum. `FINDINGS_1.md` keeps the distinction:
  writing the wider claim would be a stronger conclusion than the evidence supports, and also the
  more interesting one, which is exactly why it needs guarding against.
- **A positive result is specific too.** If the liquid book does show an edge, that is evidence
  against this paper's reach, and it is said plainly, with the trial count and the cost assumptions
  beside it.
- **The first thing `Data/analyzer.ipynb` answers is the test this paper never ran:** the liquid
  quintile alone, and what is left.
- **Its mechanism may be quoted; its magnitudes may not.** Its cost estimates are of its sample and
  era, and execution costs in large-cap equities have fallen substantially since.
- **Does not settle** the debate: it is contested, and the counterweight is not yet read. Korajczyk
  & Sadka (2004) ask how much capital momentum absorbs before costs erase it, and reach a less
  absolute conclusion; it is a **lead** in [`BIBLIOGRAPHY.md`](../BIBLIOGRAPHY.md) with no note, so
  nothing may be claimed on it until somebody reads it — the highest-value next task in this folder.
